Every line is a measurement. The contours are the elevation of the ground at four heights. The lakes are the places with no slope, at an elevation a lake sits at. The rivers were found by pointing every cell downhill and letting one unit of rain fall on all of them.

The Jordan River runs north because the ground says so — not because anybody remembered that it does.

Which matters for one reason: the drawing can be checked. Anybody with the same public elevation tiles gets the same lines in the same places, forever. No seed, no model, no interpretation in the geometry.

How it was made Eight steps, and only one of them is a choice. Learn more
  1. Download the ground

    208 elevation tiles from the public terrain set, stitched into 3,328 × 4,096 samples about 57 metres apart. Height is packed into pixel colour: R×256 + G + B/256 − 32768 metres.

  2. Find the water by looking for flatness

    A lake is the only thing in a landscape with no slope. The elevation half of that test is load-bearing — flatness alone hands back the Bonneville Salt Flats, because at this resolution a lake and the memory of a lake are the same shape.

  3. Trace the contours

    Marching squares, interpolated between samples rather than stepping between them, so the line lands where the height actually crosses.

  4. Fill every hollow, but leave a whisper of slope

    Water in an unfilled pit stops. Fill the pit flat and it stops again, because flat ground has no downhill. Priority-flood with a hair of epsilon does both. Without it: 201,709 dead cells and not one river. With it: 422.

  5. Let it rain, then decide where a channel begins

    Channels do not initiate on area — they initiate on stress, which goes as area × slope squared. Measured here: 0.115 at a canyon head against 0.001 on the valley floor. One constant is then right in both places.

  6. Walk each river down, longest first

    From every head, steepest descent until it meets a lake, an edge, or a channel already drawn — so a tributary joins a trunk instead of replacing it.

  7. Cut the corners

    Three passes of Chaikin. The limit of that process is a quadratic B-spline — you do not differentiate a staircase, you integrate it.

  8. Write curves, not pixels

    Douglas–Peucker discards every point the curve does not need, then each chain becomes cubic Béziers with tangents from its neighbours.

The smooth pass why it is vector and not a bigger picture

Every earlier version was a raster, and a raster has one resolution. The jagged curves at 500% were never a fault in the drawing — that is what a curve looks like after it has been committed to a grid and magnified. You cannot smooth it afterwards; the information went when the grid took it.

So this goes back to the elevation and never lands on a grid at all. The tilt is applied to the coordinates rather than to a bitmap — a rotated raster resamples and loses; a rotated coordinate is just a coordinate.

No potable water to cool a machine. No consuming basin water to cool a machine — and in a terminal basin that is the one that matters. Bring 150% of your own peak load in renewable generation. Hold thirty-six hours of storage. Sign a grid agreement with the utility that serves you, and get paid for it.

It does not ask where your water came from. Truck it, pipe it, distil it, buy it in jugs. The chapter says so in a subsection.

Arizona has required a hundred-year assured water supply before you can build a subdivision since 1980. California has required solar on new homes since 2020. This asks a data centre for both. There is nothing new in it, which is the entire point.

Dust the evaporative loss bill732 words →

Three numbers, all of them already on file: water in, water out, and your water bill. In minus out is what you destroyed. If the bill already covers it at the rate every commercial buyer here pays, you owe nothing — put the water back, or send it out inside a product, and nothing changes at all: the market stall, the dairy, the greenhouse, the brewery.

It never names a crop, and it never asks where anything goes. The charge applies “without regard to the destination, market, or purchaser of any product… the product, crop, animal, beverage, container or process involved.” The bill does not know what alfalfa is, and it does not know what a state line is either. Evaporation simply happens to be the thing that never comes back.

And it takes nothing. Section six says the right is not limited, conditioned, curtailed, forfeited or reduced; no crop is prohibited; no application may be denied by reason of it. Your rights are fine. You just cannot evaporate them at a discount.

Not a tax and not a tariff — a floor. Anyone already paying retail for what they evaporate is compliant on the day it passes. The only thing it ends is buying destruction at a discount, one written down in 1903, for an economy that grew its own food, and never revisited.

Spurs the appropriation1,478 words →

The other two take the discount away. This one pays for the transition — because the transition is what bankrupts every farmer who has ever tried it, and every water-retirement scheme in the West has died on that one sentence.

The state buys the capital cost of converting an irrigated acre, lends the rest at three percent, and then leases the water that conversion frees, every year, instream, all the way down to the lake. Three tiers. The cheapest one buys an acre-foot for $129 against $609 to $25,000 to purchase the right outright.

It never names a crop either. The tiers are acre-feet of verified depletion reduction and the capital each conversion needs — not what gets planted. A bill that pays for strawberries is a bill about strawberries, and it dies in committee alongside everyone who does not grow them.

Read per acre it is eighty-four percent of the ground going dry. Read per farm it is a 500-acre alfalfa outfit clearing $107,383 a year against the $47,250 it makes now — on sixteen percent of the water, with the family still standing on the ground.

⭐ It is not a pipe. A pipe is the cheapest thing in it. One trench carries water, power, fibre and sewer; the canopy over it makes the power and shades the crops; the cold in the line cools the buildings and then the people. Every one of those pays for the trench, and the trench was going to be dug for one of them anyway.

The Colorado. The megadrought. California drying up. The compact fight. The lawsuits. The Intermountain West. The dying lake. Seven names for one argument — and every one of them is downstream of a single number.

Two thousand two hundred and eighty-five million acre-feet fall on the United States every year. The Colorado carries fifteen million of them. Forty million people, seven states, a treaty with Mexico, five and a half million irrigated acres and a century of litigation — on two-thirds of one percent. Add the Great Salt Lake basin and the Rio Grande and the whole dry half of the country is still under one.

And the megadrought in your feed — the lawsuits, the crisis, the dying lake — is fighting over a tenth of that. A ninth of the little they already have.

Which is then played up ten ways from Sunday as inevitable. Climate. Doom. The new normal. Some of that is true and none of it is the reason. A basin can be adequate on the annual mean and empty in August, and the arithmetic above does not say the drought is imaginary. It says the shortfall is small enough to be a plumbing decision, and it has been treated as weather.

And the fence is the Rockies and the Sierra Nevada

Not a law. Two mountain ranges, and this basin sits between them.

The Sierra is why it is dry. It takes the Pacific moisture on its western face and leaves the Great Basin standing in the shadow. The Rockies are why the water that exists is somewhere else — everything east of the Continental Divide runs to the Gulf or the Atlantic, and no legislature decided that. The compacts are not the fence. They are the fence written down.

⭐ Which is the whole geography in one line: rain shadow on one side, a drainage divide on the other, and no outlet at the bottom. Two fences and no door.

And it is a good fence. It is why the American West argues about water in the Supreme Court instead of at a headgate with rifles — you do not go to war over water that physically cannot reach you. Good fences made good neighbours here, and made water wars a thing that happens in other countries.

But a fence that stops a war also permits a hoard. The Great Lakes Compact bans diversion out of the basin and exempts water in containers of 5.7 gallons or less — so a pipeline is unlawful and a bottling plant is not. Ten states are organising the same thing on the Mississippi. Nobody drafting either one was thinking about Utah, because the fence meant they never had to.

⭐ Which is the whole proposition, and it is not a legal one. The divide is an engineering cost. Once you are over it the rest is politics. Nobody is asking anyone to take the fence down. A pipe is a door in it.

The Mississippi carries twenty-three times what the entire dry half of this country lives on, and puts it in the Gulf.

There are 2.7 million miles of pipeline in this country for moving the things we burn. For moving water between basins there are perhaps thirty thousand. Ninety to one. We built the plumbing for the thing we set on fire and not for the thing we drink.

And the Mississippi is in no danger from a thirsty West

Four percent of one river would nearly double the water the entire arid West lives on.

Seventeen and a half million acre-feet, against the nineteen the West has now. The river would be down four percent — inside its own year-to-year variation. It would not notice, and the dry half of the country would have twice what it has.

⭐ And this project is not asking for four percent. It is asking for one million acre-feet — twenty-three hundredths of one percent of the Mississippi. A quarter of one percent, and the room hears “pipeline”.

Out here on the west side, we live off 4.4% of what a pioneer would have called waste.

And that is not a figure of speech. Under Utah law, water that reached the Great Salt Lake was not a beneficial use. It was legally waste — from the 1903 water code until HB 33 in 2022. When the law was written the lake was not at the table.

So it is not a rescue, and it was never sized like one

The business plan. Twelve sections, 16,985 words — read it →

Every figure in it is traceable to a source, and the estimates say so on the same line. That was not a style choice: the first draft came back with thirty-four invented numbers out of sixty-six, in fluent, confident prose, and the checker passed every one of them.

The finding that reorganised it: water is about seventeen percent of what a segment earns. The rest is power, cooling and heat. Which means this is not a water project with an energy business attached — it is an energy business whose working fluid happens to end up in a lake.

And the one that decides it: the asset is not price-sensitive, it is discount-rate sensitive. Fifteen and a half billion at three percent, nothing at all at seven. So it is not suitable for capital that needs a return inside a decade, and it says so on its own page rather than waiting to be told.

It also names the thing that would beat it: buying the water out is cheaper than building the pipe, at every price in the range. The answer is that a buyout divides a shrinking supply while the pipe adds to it — but the number is printed either way.

Do the castle walls resemble prison walls to you too?

They do, and it is not a figure of speech — it is the same wall. A castle wall and a prison wall have identical geometry, identical materials and identical cost. Neither can be told from the other by looking at it, measuring it, or drawing it. The only difference is which side you were standing on when it went up, and that is not a property of the wall.

A map has notation for water and for ground. It has none for a key, none for a door, and none for which side of a wall a person is standing on. The exclusion is never in the drawing. It is always in the room.

By this point every alternative is worse and the arithmetic says so. Holding the level would cost more than the entire assessed value of the shoreline it protects. Refusing to manage the decline does not prevent the decline. It only removes the schedule.

Watch the promenade for an hour and count. Runners. Dog walkers. Strollers being pushed by people who drove to the top to push them. A couple photographing the view, and somebody filming themselves doing the loop.

Almost nobody on that hill is on foot in order to arrive somewhere. They are on foot as an activity, having got there by car — which is the same arrangement as Los Angeles and reads the same way from a bench.

So the amenity is not being used as relief. It is being used as equipment.

The bench is where a runner puts a foot up to stretch. The fountain is for the dog. The level resurfaced walkway is for the pram and the running form, and the handrail is a thing to touch on the way past. Every element of it was specified for a body that is struggling and every element of it is used by a body that chose the gradient on purpose.

⭐ And it photographs beautifully, which is the last piece. The walkability is the content. The most photographed pedestrian environment in the state is the one whose pedestrians all arrived by car, forty feet above the point where the people it was built for turned around.

And the alignment is not a coincidence*

An earlier note on this page said the line follows the gardens rather than the churches. That is wrong, and it is wrong in the direction that matters.

The gradient on that hill was set by sightlines. The lots were valued by what they could see, from the 1850s, and what they could see was the temple. The view set the price; the price selected the elevation; the elevation is now the waterline. So the marina profile, the armoured bank, the dry side of the wall and the address of the best dock in the valley all descend from a religious sightline surveyed a hundred and seventy years before anybody wrote a bill.

⭐ Which is not a claim about the church and is not offered as one. The church is simply upstream of the price, and the price is upstream of the water. Any fixed object that a city agrees to look at will do the same thing — a cathedral, a harbour, a capitol, a mountain. Put a thing worth seeing at one end of a slope and the slope sorts itself by who can pay to see it, and then, a century and a half later, the lake arrives and sorts the same people again in the same order using nothing but elevation.
